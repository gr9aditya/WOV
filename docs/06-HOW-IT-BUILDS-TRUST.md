# How it builds trust

Trust in an election system is not a feeling to be produced. It is a judgement a
person is entitled to make once they can check something for themselves.

This game does not try to make anyone trust e-voting. It tries to make them
**capable of checking**, and treats whatever they conclude afterwards as
legitimate. A voter who plays this and comes away sceptical but able to verify is
a success. A voter who comes away reassured but unable to verify is a failure,
even though they like the system more.

That distinction is the whole document.

## Three layers

### Cognitive — knowing what the check is for

A voter who does not know why a field asks for a code cannot tell a broken process
from a working one. Every step in the game is paired with a learn card naming the
real step **and the threat it defeats** — the second half being the part leaflets
usually omit.

Knowing that the boat number must match is instruction. Knowing that a wrong boat
means a forged card or a phishing site is understanding, and only the second
transfers to a situation the game did not depict.

### Emotional — having felt the consequence

The cost of skipping a check is abstract until it happens to you. In the game it
happens in two seconds: the wrong boat puts out, breaks up, and Bruno goes under.

That is not decoration and it is not punishment — it is the only part of the
experience that makes the check feel worth doing. Afterwards the player does not
*remember being told* verification matters. They remember the boat.

The emotional layer is also why the ending is bright. Finalisation is what lets
you stop worrying, so the game ends in daylight. Trust is the feeling of having
checked, not the feeling of having been reassured.

### Procedural — having done it, in order

Cognitive and emotional understanding still leave a voter who has never performed
the sequence. The game gives them one complete run: private window, card, verify,
initialise, compare returned codes, confirm, finalise, clean up.

When they meet the real portal it is not the first time. That is the difference
between recognition and recall, and it is worth more than either of the other two
layers.

## Why understanding is what makes trust legitimate

There is a shortcut available here and this project refuses it.

The shortcut is reassurance: tell people the system is secure, show credentials,
cite audits. It sometimes works, and it produces trust that cannot survive
contact with a real anomaly — because a voter who trusts on authority has no way
to tell a genuine problem from a rumour. Their confidence is brittle in exactly the
moment it matters.

The alternative is slower. Give the voter the ability to check one thing
themselves — that what the system recorded matches what they intended — and their
confidence rests on something they can re-verify at will. That is what individual
verifiability *is*, and it is why Swiss Post's system is built the way it is.

**A voter who can check does not need to be reassured.** The game's job is to
produce that voter.

## The narrator's voice

Rules the writing follows, because tone can undo the design.

- **Never say "don't worry."** The correct response to an unverified process is
  worry. Say what to check.
- **Name the threat, plainly.** Not "for your security" but "someone could open a
  session in your name."
- **Do not claim perfection.** The game never says e-voting is safe. It says these
  checks defeat these attacks.
- **The player is competent.** Failure is a step to repeat, never a scolding.
- **The paper is the authority, never the screen.** Consistently, everywhere. It
  is the one habit worth transferring whole.

## Trust that would be a failure

Ways this could "work" while doing harm:

| Outcome | Why it is bad |
|---|---|
| "It's a game, so it must be simple and safe" | Trivialises a real threat model |
| "I trust it because it looked official" | Authority-based trust, brittle |
| "I don't need to check, the system checks" | Inverts the lesson exactly |
| "I verified in the game, so I'm safe now" | The game verifies nothing |

The last is the one to watch. The closing panel is explicit that the checks belong
to the real portal, not to Bruno's world.

## How you would know it worked

Not "did they enjoy it" but:

- Do they **compare against paper** unprompted?
- Can they **name a threat** for a given step?
- Do they **slow down at the returned codes** on the real portal?
- If shown a portal that skips finalisation, do they **notice something missing**?

That last question is the real test, and the honest position is that it has not
been run. This is a hypothesis with a design behind it, not a demonstrated result.
